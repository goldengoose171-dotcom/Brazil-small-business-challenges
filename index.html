A primer for multi-unit operators

Why "unit economics" means something different in Brazil

A US franchisee looks at a cafe and sees a build-out, a lease, a crew and a margin. A Brazilian franchisee sees the same four things plus three invisible partners: a central bank charging 13.75%, a tax code that roughly doubles its take past ~R$4.8M in revenue, and a labor code from 1943. This primer works backward from that reality to the economics and history that produced it.

00 · The question

Why is capital so scarce for small business in Brazil?

Ask a Brazilian operator and the answer is not the one a US franchisee expects. It is not bureaucracy, and it is not the franchisor. It is the price of money and the shape of the tax code, which together make the same store worth much less to the person who owns it.

Observation 1

Rates, not regulation

Interest rates are the binding constraint. They make no sense against the thin margins of a small business, so debt-financed growth rarely works.

Observation 2

A cliff at about US$1M

Past roughly US$1M in revenue the tax rate roughly doubles, and the threshold follows the person, so every business an owner holds is added together.

Observation 3

Few multi-unit owners

The usual workaround is spreading ownership across family members, which runs out fast. The result is very few multi-unit franchisees and almost none past five units.

Each observation is directionally right

The rest of this page explains the mechanism behind each one, the economic levers involved, and why the country arrived at these laws in the first place.

01 · First principles

A business is a machine. The state controls four of its dials.

Strip away franchise agreements and brand standards and any cafe is the same machine: it borrows capital, hires labor, converts both into sales, and keeps what is left after the state takes its share. A government cannot legislate demand, but it sets the price of every input on the way through.

The first-principles insight

Interest and revenue-based taxes are both fixed claims that do not shrink when margins do. A business with a 15% operating margin can survive one of them. In Brazil it faces both, so the survivable margin floor is far higher, and the range of concepts that "work" as franchises is far narrower. That is what "unit economics has a different meaning" means in practice.

02 · Lever 1

Cost of capital: why Brazil has the world's most expensive money

Brazil's real interest rate (policy rate minus expected inflation) has sat near the top of the world for three decades. In 2025 the Selic hit 15%; in September 2026 it was cut for the fifth straight meeting to 13.75%. A small business does not borrow at Selic. It borrows at Selic plus a spread that is itself among the widest on earth.

Layer 1

Selic · 13.75%

The risk-free floor. Every real in the system can earn this in overnight government paper, so a bank must beat it to lend to you at all.

Layer 2

Bank spread · +8 to +25 pts

Average corporate working-capital loans ran ~23% in 2026. Five banks hold most of the market; recovery on defaulted collateral is slow and litigious; taxes on financial operations (IOF) and reserve requirements are baked in.

Layer 3

Small-firm premium · 30–60%+

Unsecured lines, overdrafts (cheque especial) and receivables anticipation carry rates that would be usury elsewhere. This is where a first-time franchisee actually lives.

Why? Five root causes, in causal order

1 · Hyperinflation left a scar the system still prices

Between 1980 and 1994 Brazil went through five currencies and annual inflation that exceeded 2,000%. The Plano Real (1994) killed it, but only by making the central bank credibly willing to keep rates brutally high. Every subsequent inflation scare (2002, 2015, 2021–22) reinforced the reflex. Inflation expectations remain "unanchored" in the central bank's own language, so the neutral real rate the bank believes it needs (~5%) is double what most peers assume.

2 · Indexation never fully died

Rents, wages, pensions, utility tariffs and much government spending are still legally linked to past inflation. When prices rise, costs mechanically follow, so a rate hike has to be larger to bite. This is why Brazil's central bank moves in bigger steps and holds longer than the Fed.

3 · Fiscal dominance: the state is the biggest borrower

Gross public debt sits around three-quarters of GDP, much of it short-dated and floating-rate (linked to Selic). The 1988 Constitution hard-wired spending growth (pensions, health, education floors) that Congress cannot easily cut. A government that must roll over enormous debt every year competes with every cafe for the same savings. The market prices the risk that it will inflate its way out, which keeps the rate high, which raises interest costs, which widens the deficit. That loop is the core of the problem.

4 · Earmarked credit crowds out free credit

Roughly 40% of Brazilian credit is "directed": subsidized housing, agriculture and BNDES development loans at below-market rates, funded by mandatory allocations. Banks compensate by charging more on the "free" segment, which is exactly where small business borrows. Subsidized credit for favored sectors is paid for by unsubsidized credit for everyone else.

5 · Weak creditor recovery and a concentrated banking system

Bankruptcy resolution in Brazil has historically taken years with cents-on-the-dollar recovery, so lenders price every loan as if it will default. Combine that with a market where a handful of banks control most assets, and there is little competitive pressure on spreads. Pix (2020), open finance and fintech lenders are eroding this, but slowly.

03 · Lever 2

The tax cliff: "it doubles after US$1M, and it's by person"

The "doubles at US$1M, by person" observation maps almost exactly onto one law: Complementary Law 123 of 2006, which created Simples Nacional. Understanding why that law exists explains both the gift and the trap.

What Simples is

A single monthly payment that replaces up to eight federal, state and municipal taxes (IRPJ, CSLL, PIS, COFINS, IPI, ICMS, ISS and, crucially, the employer's 20% social-security contribution on payroll). Rates are progressive on gross revenue, roughly 4% to 19% for commerce/food service. It applies to companies with up to R$4.8M in trailing-12-month revenue, which is roughly US$0.9–1.0M at recent exchange rates. That is the "US$1M" line operators talk about.

Why it is "by person"

Article 3 of the law disqualifies any company whose partner holds more than 10% of another company if the combined gross revenue of all such companies exceeds R$4.8M. The cap follows the owner, not the CNPJ (tax ID). Open a second cafe under a second company and the revenues are summed. Exceed the limit and exclusion can be retroactive, with back taxes, interest and penalties. Hence the family workaround: a spouse or adult child as the sole owner of the next unit resets the counter, once per person.

Mechanism of the jump: inside Simples the employer social-security charge is inside the single rate. Outside it, the company pays Lucro Presumido taxes on revenue (roughly 9–10% for food service after PIS/COFINS, IRPJ, CSLL and ICMS at typical regimes) plus a separate ~28–30% employer charge on the entire payroll (INSS 20%, FGTS 8%, RAT and "third-party" contributions). For a labor-heavy business that second piece is what makes the total look like it doubled: measured against the mid-range Simples rate most growing operators actually pay, the all-in bill rises 1.6–2× at the line. Figures are simplified; state ICMS regimes and municipal rules vary widely.

Why the law was written this way

1988

The "Citizen Constitution"

Post-dictatorship Brazil wrote generous social rights into the constitution: universal health, pensions, education spending floors. It also told the state to give small businesses "differentiated and favored treatment." Both promises had to be funded. Tax burden climbed from ~24% of GDP in 1990 to ~33% by the 2000s, mostly via taxes on consumption and payroll rather than income, because those are easier to collect in an economy with vast informality.

1996–2006

Informality as the problem to solve

By the early 2000s roughly half of Brazilian workers were informal and millions of businesses simply never registered. The state's answer was not to lower the general burden (it could not, given the constitutional spending) but to carve out a cheap on-ramp: Simples Federal (1996), then Simples Nacional (2006) unifying all three levels of government. The goal was formalization, not scale.

2006–2018

The cliff is a design choice, not an accident

Every subsidized regime needs a boundary, and every boundary needs an anti-abuse rule. The per-person aggregation exists precisely to stop one owner from splitting a large business into many small ones. The cap was raised (R$2.4M → R$3.6M → R$4.8M in 2018) but the shape stayed: a sharp step rather than a smooth phase-out. Legislators optimized for the millions of micro firms entering the system, not the thousands trying to grow past it.

2019–2024

The tax wedge becomes visible

Franchising boomed inside the cap (Brazil now has one of the largest franchise sectors on earth by brand count) while remaining overwhelmingly single-unit. The economic term is a notch: a threshold where the average tax rate jumps discontinuously. Firms bunch just below it, split legally across relatives, or stop growing. Economists call this a "size-dependent distortion"; it shows up in Brazil's data as a missing middle of mid-sized firms.

2023–2033

Reform, partially

Constitutional Amendment 132 (2023) replaces five consumption taxes with a dual VAT (CBS federal, IBS state/municipal) at a combined standard rate estimated around 26.5–28%. 2026 is a 1% test year; the old taxes phase out by 2033. Simples survives, but firms inside it will face pressure from business customers who want VAT credits. The notch is not removed; its edges are being redrawn.

Why this caps multi-unit franchising

A US operator scales by consolidating: one entity, one balance sheet, one line of credit secured against many units. In Brazil the tax law rewards the opposite: fragment ownership across people to stay under the notch. Fragmented ownership cannot be collateralized cleanly, cannot centralize purchasing without triggering related-party scrutiny, and runs out of relatives. Five units at ~R$1M each is about where the arithmetic of a family runs out, which is exactly what operators on the ground report.

04 · Lever 3

The cost of a person

The Consolidação das Leis do Trabalho (CLT) dates to 1943, written under Getúlio Vargas on a corporatist model borrowed from 1930s Italy. It treats the employment relationship as something the state guarantees rather than something two parties negotiate. Eighty years of case law and a 2017 reform have modified it, not replaced it.

Why the state chose this

Vargas needed an urban industrial workforce loyal to the state rather than to unions or communists. Guaranteeing rights by statute, funded by employers, was the bargain. The 1988 Constitution then elevated many of those rights (FGTS, 13th, vacation premium, 44-hour week) to constitutional status, putting them beyond ordinary legislation. Labor courts became one of the busiest judicial systems in the world, with millions of claims a year; a franchisee with 20 staff should expect to be sued periodically as a cost of doing business.

The economic lever it pulls

A high, fixed cost per formal worker pushes marginal jobs into informality or into "PJ" contracting (workers incorporating as one-person companies). It also raises the return to automation and self-service, which is why Brazilian retail and food service adopted kiosks, apps and Pix payments faster than the US. The 2017 reform allowed negotiated agreements to override some statutory rules and made losing plaintiffs pay costs; claims fell sharply but the cost structure remained.

05 · Lever 4

Compliance friction: the tax you pay in hours

Brazil has 27 state VAT (ICMS) regimes, over 5,500 municipal service taxes (ISS), cumulative federal levies, and an electronic invoicing system that reports every sale to the tax authority in real time. This is why Brazilian operators say "regulation" is not the root problem: it is real, but it is a symptom of the fiscal machine, not its cause.

Federalism without coordination. The 1988 Constitution gave states and municipalities their own taxing powers to fund devolved responsibilities. Each level then used its tax as an industrial-policy tool: states offered ICMS breaks to lure factories (the "fiscal war"), municipalities competed on ISS. Complexity is the residue of thirty years of competitive exemptions.

Cumulative taxes are easy to collect. Taxes on gross revenue at every stage of a supply chain (rather than value added) are simple for a revenue-hungry state to administer and hard to evade with electronic invoicing, but they cascade: a cafe pays tax on inputs that were already taxed. That is why the same headline rates produce a higher effective burden than in a VAT country.

Real-time surveillance as a substitute for trust. Since the NF-e electronic invoice (2008) and the SPED digital bookkeeping system, every invoice is validated by the state before it is legally issued. The upside is that Brazil's tax authority is one of the most technologically advanced in the world. The downside is that every business needs an accountant on retainer and enterprise-grade software from day one.

2026

Test year

CBS 0.9% + IBS 0.1% charged and fully credited against existing taxes. Zero net burden; the point is to force every company's invoicing system to handle the new fields.

2027

CBS goes live

Federal PIS/COFINS abolished; CBS replaces them at roughly 8.8%. A "selective tax" on harmful goods (alcohol, tobacco, sugary drinks) begins.

2029–2032

IBS phases in

State ICMS and municipal ISS shrink by steps each year while IBS grows to fill the gap.

2033

Dual VAT complete

Single non-cumulative base, full input credits, destination principle. Combined standard rate estimated 26.5–28%, among the highest in the world, because the reform is revenue-neutral by design: it changes the shape of the burden, not its size.

What it means for a franchisee

Cascading disappears, which lowers effective rates for businesses with long supply chains. But Simples firms cannot pass full VAT credits to business customers, so B2B-heavy concepts (catering, corporate accounts) will feel pressure to leave Simples early. The notch does not go away; the reform does nothing to the R$4.8M line or the per-person rule.

06 · The system

How the loops lock together

None of the four levers is a policy mistake in isolation. Each is a rational response to the one before it. That is what makes the system stable, and why it has survived left and right governments alike.

The reinforcing loop (red)

Constitutional spending → structural deficit → heavy debt issuance → high Selic to keep inflation credible → higher interest on that same debt → bigger deficit. Interest alone consumes a larger share of Brazil's budget than health or education. Every attempt to break it (spending caps in 2016, the fiscal framework of 2023) has been softened by Congress within a few years.

The balancing loops (green)

Informality is the economy's escape valve; Simples is the state's attempt to close it; the notch is Simples' side effect; family fragmentation is the entrepreneur's answer to the notch. Each loop stabilizes the system at a low-growth, small-firm equilibrium. Growth-oriented operators are the ones the equilibrium is not designed for.

So, is regulation the root problem?

No. The root is a fiscal contract written in 1988 that promised more than the tax base could fund without either high inflation or high real interest rates. Brazil chose high real rates in 1994 and has paid for that choice every year since. Regulation, the tax stack and the labor code are the plumbing built around that contract. Fixing the plumbing (the 2026–2033 reform) helps; it does not change the water pressure.

What is changing, and what is not

Improving

Cost of money

Selic in a cutting cycle (15% → 13.75%), Pix and open finance compressing bank spreads, fintech lenders underwriting on receivables data rather than collateral.

Mixed

Tax structure

Cascading and the state-by-state maze are being dismantled through 2033. The standard rate will be very high, and the Simples notch and per-person rule remain untouched.

Unchanged

The fiscal contract

Constitutional spending floors and pension growth still outrun revenue. Until that changes, the neutral real rate stays high and the whole stack stays expensive.

One-sentence takeaway

In the US, scale is the reward for good unit economics; in Brazil, scale is taxed and financed as if it were a risk, so the system selects for many small owners of few units rather than few owners of many, and any operator entering the market has to design around that rather than through it.
